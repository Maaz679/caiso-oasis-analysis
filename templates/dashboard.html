The LMP is the price CAISO sets every five minutes for buying or selling one additional megawatt-hour of electricity at a specific location on the grid. It reflects what it would actually cost the system to serve one more unit of demand right now, not an average or a contract rate.

The price is the sum of four components. Energy is set by the fuel and operating cost of the most expensive generating unit currently dispatched system-wide. Congestion appears whenever a transmission line hits its physical limit; it can be positive (line is constrained against you) or negative (constraint works in your favor). Loss accounts for electricity that dissipates as heat during transmission; also signed. GHG (Carbon) reflects California's cap-and-trade carbon price, which adds a fixed cost per MWh whenever the carbon market is active. When GHG is non-zero, it is typically the second-largest component after Energy.

The bottom panel shows each component as its own line so you can see when any of them goes negative or spikes independently of the others.

CAISO operates a single interconnected grid, but prices differ across the state because of physical constraints on the transmission network. The three trading hubs shown here are aggregated pricing nodes that represent large geographic zones rather than single substations.

NP15 covers northern California, roughly the PG&E service territory north of a dividing line near Fresno. It tends to have lower prices when hydropower from the Sierra Nevada and Pacific Northwest imports are abundant. SP15 covers southern California under SCE, a large load center with high summer air conditioning demand and proximity to desert solar resources. ZP26 covers the SDG&E territory around San Diego, a smaller and often more expensive zone because it is electrically isolated and depends heavily on import capacity through a few key transmission corridors.

The spread chart in the lower panel shows each hub's price relative to the real-time system average. A persistent positive spread in one zone signals a local supply shortage or transmission constraint isolating that area. A negative spread means that zone has cheaper local resources than the rest of the grid. Path 26 between NP15 and SP15 and the Southwest Powerlink into ZP26 are the two most commonly congested interfaces in California, and their impact shows up directly in these spreads.

California's grid draws from over a dozen fuel types simultaneously, and their relative contributions shift dramatically over the course of a day. The top panel stacks each source's output, so the total height of the chart at any moment equals total in-state generation plus net imports. The area is not fixed at 100 percent because total generation rises and falls with demand.

The pattern known as the Duck Curve is visible whenever the window covers daylight hours. Solar output (gold) climbs steeply in the morning and peaks around noon, sometimes pushing total renewable generation high enough to displace nearly all other sources. Then solar drops off sharply after 3 or 4 PM. At the same time, residential demand rises as people return home in the evening. The grid must ramp up Natural Gas (red) peakers extremely fast to fill that gap, often adding several thousand megawatts within an hour. That ramp is one of the most operationally challenging aspects of running a high-solar grid.

Nuclear and Large Hydro run steadily around the clock because they are either inflexible (nuclear) or operationally efficient to hold at a constant output (hydro). Imports (gray) reflect net power flowing into California from neighboring states and are often highest in the early morning when in-state demand is low and out-of-state generators have surplus capacity to sell cheaply.

The bottom panel shows battery storage behavior. Bars below zero mean batteries are charging, which typically happens mid-day when solar is abundant and prices are low. Bars above zero mean batteries are discharging into the evening peak. California now has several gigawatts of grid-scale battery capacity installed, and its charging and discharging behavior directly flattens the Duck Curve by absorbing excess solar and releasing it during the ramp.

This chart shows each fuel type's average contribution to California's total generation over the past 12 hours. The center displays the overall average output in gigawatts.

Because the mix is averaged across the full window, it smooths out the intraday volatility shown in the stacked chart. A 12-hour average that includes overnight hours will understate solar's midday contribution and overstate how much gas was running at all times. To understand the current carbon intensity of the grid, you need to look at the stacked chart in real time rather than this average. Still, the portfolio view is useful for understanding the rough structural balance: how much of California's power comes from carbon-free sources (solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, geothermal) versus fossil sources (natural gas, coal) on a given day.

California's renewable portfolio standard requires utilities to procure a growing percentage of power from eligible renewables. The share shown here reflects actual dispatch, which can differ from contracted capacity because renewable output depends on weather conditions.

System load is the total amount of electricity being consumed across California at each moment, measured in megawatts. This figure comes from CAISO's real-time measurement of actual demand flowing through the grid, not a forecast.

California's load follows a predictable daily shape. Demand is at its lowest between 2 and 5 AM when most households and businesses are idle. It climbs through the morning as commercial and industrial facilities open, plateaus through midday, and then peaks in the early evening, typically between 5 and 9 PM, when residential demand combines with remaining commercial load. Air conditioning adds a significant seasonal overlay: summer loads can be 30 to 40 percent higher than winter loads on hot days, and extreme heat waves have pushed California's all-time peak above 52,000 MW.

CAISO is responsible for balancing generation against this load curve in real time, every five minutes. Generating too little causes frequency to drop, which can damage equipment and trigger automatic blackouts. Generating too much causes frequency to rise, which can force generators to trip off-line. The grid operates within a narrow frequency band of 59.95 to 60.05 Hz, and maintaining that balance as load shifts by thousands of megawatts over the course of a day is the core operational challenge CAISO solves continuously.
