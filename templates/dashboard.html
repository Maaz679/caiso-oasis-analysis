About This Dashboard

This dashboard shows real-time data from CAISO (California Independent System Operator), the organization that manages California's power grid and electricity market. CAISO ensures that electricity supply matches demand 24/7 across the state.

The data below updates every 10 minutes and shows: electricity prices, where power comes from (solar, wind, natural gas, etc.), and how much electricity Californians are using right now. Each chart includes an explanation to help you understand what you're looking at.

1

Locational Marginal Price (LMP)

What you're seeing: Two charts showing electricity prices. The top chart shows the total price over time. The bottom chart breaks that price into three stacked components.

The three components: Energy (green) is the basic cost of generating power. Congestion (orange) is added when power lines are crowded and electricity can't flow freely. Loss (red) accounts for electricity lost during transmission over long distances.

Why it matters: The total price changes throughout the day based on demand and available power plants. When you see more orange or red in the bottom chart, it means transmission issues are making electricity more expensive.
2

Trading Hub Comparison

What you're seeing: Two charts comparing electricity prices across three major California regions: NP15 (Northern California), SP15 (Southern California), and ZP26 (San Diego area). The top chart shows actual prices, the bottom shows the price difference from the state average.

Why it matters: Prices vary across California because different regions have different power plants and demand patterns. The bottom chart makes it easy to see which areas are paying more or less than average.

What to look for: When one region's line is far above the others, that area has higher electricity costs, usually due to transmission constraints or local supply shortages.
3

Generation Mix and Duck Curve

What you're seeing: Two charts showing California's power sources. The top chart is a stacked area showing where electricity comes from hour by hour. The bottom chart shows battery storage activity (charging when negative, discharging when positive).

The famous duck curve: Watch solar (yellow) spike during the day and disappear at sunset. Natural gas (red) has to quickly ramp up when solar drops off. Nuclear and hydro (blue and teal) provide steady power throughout the day.

Why batteries matter: The bottom chart shows batteries charging during the day when solar is abundant (dip below zero) and discharging in the evening when needed (rise above zero).
4

Average Generation Portfolio

What you're seeing: A pie chart showing what percentage of California's electricity came from each source, averaged over the past 12 hours.

Why it matters: This gives you a quick snapshot of California's energy mix. Larger slices mean that source provided more power. Look for how much comes from renewables (solar, wind, hydro) versus fossil fuels (natural gas).

Note: This is an average - the actual mix changes dramatically throughout the day. For example, solar might be 40% at noon but 0% at midnight.
5

System Load Profile

What you're seeing: How much electricity California is using right now, measured in megawatts. This is total demand across the entire state.

The daily pattern: Demand is low at night when people are asleep. It rises in the morning, stays high during the day, and typically peaks in the evening when people come home, cook dinner, and use appliances. In summer, peaks are higher due to air conditioning.

Why it matters: Power plants must generate exactly this much electricity at all times. Too little and you get blackouts. Too much and you waste energy. The grid must balance supply and demand perfectly every second.
